da6idb.batch

Gridding a run of scans onto one common lattice.

expand_scans(start, end, step=1)

The scan numbers from start to end inclusive, every step.

Given 152, 160, 2 this is [152, 154, 156, 158, 160]. The ends may be the other way round, and a step of zero is taken to mean one.

parse_scans(text)

Scan numbers from what someone typed: "152, 154 157-160".

find_scans(folder)

{scan number: image path} for the pva files in folder.

grid_scans(folder, numbers, hkl_range=None, oversample=1.0, output=None, step=None, reuse=True, normalize_transmission=True, on_start=None, on_geometry=None, on_done=None, progress=None)

Grid several scans onto one lattice. Returns [(number, path, note)].

path is None for a scan that could not be done, and note says why. The cell size comes from the first scan that reads successfully unless step is given outright. With normalize_transmission each scan's frames are divided by its attenuator transmission where that varies, which is decided per scan -- a series can mix scans that moved their filter with scans that did not.

da6idb.combine

Telling repeats of one measurement apart, and putting them together.

baseline_values(path, fields)

The baseline's own reading of each of fields that it carries.

Before the scan, not after: where the sample sat while it ran.

collect_uniques(values, atol)

The distinct settings in values, counting any two within atol as one.

process_data.collect_uniques verbatim. A readback never repeats exactly, so the settings have to be found by distance; the first value of each cluster is the one kept, which is why the result follows the sorted order rather than the order measured.

scan_signature(path)

What a scan has to share with another before it can be a repeat of it.

The plan and the motors it stepped, taken from the metadata rather than the columns: two scans that moved different things are not repeats of one another however alike their numbers look. None where the scan stepped nothing -- a count has no shape to repeat, and the 32 of them in one directory here would otherwise read as a single measurement taken 32 times, their row counts running from 1 to 600.

sample_atol(name)

How far name may move with the sample still counting as unmoved.

scan_groups(paths)

Gather the selection into the sets that are one measurement repeated.

Three things have to agree. The plan and the motors, from scan_signature. Where the sample sat and how cold it was, read from the baseline through SAMPLE_STAGES -- which is what tells repeats from a series, 174, 175 and 176 being the same energy scan with the sample stepped 0.2 mm between them. And the points themselves, which only the curves can answer and same_grid settles later.

Each quantity is clustered across the selection with collect_uniques at its own tolerance and every scan then assigned to the nearest surviving value, so the grouping does not depend on which scan happened to be looked at first. Groups come back in the order their first scan appears, which is the table's own order.

A scan whose baseline carries no whole stage is left on its own rather than pooled on the temperature alone. That is the conservative way round: an endstation nobody has listed here draws its scans separately, which is what the viewer does today, instead of averaging a series into one curve that looks clean and is not one measurement.

curve_step(x)

The typical distance between one point of x and the next.

The median rather than the span over the count: a list scan need not step evenly, and one long jump back to the start would make every real step look small beside it. Zero where nothing moved.

same_grid(x_a, x_b)

Whether two scans measured the same points, to within half a step.

Compared row by row over the overlap, so a scan stopped part way is still the same grid as far as it got. The tolerance is a fraction of the scan's own step rather than an absolute distance, there being no one figure that suits an energy in keV and an angle in degrees at once; see SAME_X_STEPS for where the half comes from.

stack_mean(pieces, rows)

The mean of pieces row by row over rows, counting only the finite.

A measurement stopped part way leaves its last rows short, so a row only some of the pieces reached is the mean of those and a row none of them reached comes back NaN -- never a short piece quietly read as zero. Shared by the repeats within one scan and the repeats across several, which are the same reduction asked for twice.

combine_curves(x_a, y_a, x_b, y_b, subtract)

A + B, or A - B, with B read onto A's X by interpolation.

Two scans of one thing do not step the same points. Scans 171 and 173 are 109 energies each and differ by up to 4e-05 keV -- the readback, sampled at two different moments -- so adding them point by point would be adding readings taken at different energies.

Only across the overlap. Left to itself interp holds B's first and last readings flat right across A, drawing a sum out where there is no second scan at all; left and right make that stretch NaN and the line simply stops. On 171 and 173 that is the first point and the last, 171 starting above 173 and ending below it.

The sort is what makes this correct rather than merely tidy. interp documents xp as increasing and says nothing whatever when it is not -- and both of those scans run downwards in energy, as does any scan stepped from the top of an edge. Handed 173 as it comes off disk it returns 0 finite points out of 109, so the sum would be a blank curve on exactly the pair this was written for; without the NaN guards it is instead wrong by 64452 on values around 4e5, in silence. An averaged curve's X can also carry the NaN pad group_curve leaves, which the same clean removes.

combine_name(first, second, subtract)

How a combination reads, in the menus and on the plot alike.

Writing a slice or a cut out to a file.

slug(text)

A label that is safe in a file name or an HDF5 key.

suffix_for(path, selected='')

Which writer to use: the extension if it says, else the chosen filter.

write_matrix(path, image, x, y, x_label='x', y_label='y', description=(), suffix=None)

A 2D array with its two axes. Unmeasured cells are written as NaN.

table_columns(curves, x_label='x')

{heading: values} for curves, laid out side by side.

curves is [(label, x, y), ...]. Curves drawn against the **same X array** share one X column between them, so three detectors of one scan come out as psic_eta, det1, det2, det3 rather than as three repeats of the same angles.

The same array, not merely equal values, and the difference matters. Two scans of one thing read their motor at two different moments and differ in the fifth decimal: those are two honest X columns. Comparing values would weld them together, would weld any two one-point scans, and would never collapse an averaged curve, whose X can carry a NaN pad and so is not even equal to itself. Reading a scan's X once and handing the same array to each of its fields is what the caller already does, so identity answers exactly the question being asked -- were these drawn against one sampling.

Columns come back at their own lengths; write_columns squares them off.

datasets is [(name, note, {column: values}), ...]. Text and CSV take one file per cut, suffixing the name when there is more than one; the archive formats hold them all.

da6idb.files

Finding the files a scan is made of, and pairing them up.

detector_files_from_metadata(master)

The detector file paths the master records, in the order it lists them.

The value reads lambda250k: /some/where/scan_00001.h5, one detector per line; older masters write an empty {} there, which yields nothing.

image_path_for_master(master)

The image file belonging to master, or None if none is next to it.

Tried in turn: the detector file the master names -- where it points, then relative to the experiment root, then by name beside the master -- the file_name in the older pva_streaming block, and last the <master stem minus "_master"> -> pva_<stem>.h5 convention. Newer masters name a file that follows no convention at all (test_00001.h5 beside test_00001_master.hdf), which only the recorded name can find.

master_path_for_image(image)

The master file this image file belongs to.

Beside the image first. Failing that, a short walk up the tree, trying each ancestor and each ancestor's copy of the image's own directory name -- which is where the master sits when the detector writes into a parallel tree. The name is specific enough (<scan>_master.hdf) that the search stays safe; callers that already know the pair should pass it rather than rely on this.

read_master_rois(master)

The detector's ROIs as recorded in the master's baseline.

Newer image files carry no entry/data/rois of their own, so this is the only record of where the detector's ROIs were. The numbers are the same ones the older writer put in the image file -- a corner and a size in pixels -- so they come back in the same shape.

is_image_file(path)

Whether path is itself an image file (i.e. it holds an image stack).

resolve_image_file(path)

The image file to open for path, which may be a master or an image file.

Selecting a pva*.h5 directly in the file list is the obvious way to ask for it, so that opens itself rather than hunting for a partner.

scan_number(path)

The scan number in a file name: 00008 from tise2_2_00008_master.hdf.

The last run of digits wins, so the prefix's own digits (tise2_2) are ignored and the sibling pva_tise2_2_00008.h5 reports the same number.

scan_tags(paths)

A short name per path, keyed by str(path): enough to tell them apart.

The scan number alone normally does it. Where it does not -- two series in one directory whose numbers collide, scan_number taking the last run of digits either way -- every name gains its series. The file listing gets away with the bare number because its other six columns disambiguate; a bare menu of numbers would not.

file_prefix(path)

The part of a file name in front of the scan number: tise2_2.

Everything from the scan number onwards is dropped, so the files of one series all reduce to the same string.

list_hdf_files(directory)

Return the HDF5 files directly inside directory, sorted by name.

da6idb.fit

Fitting a peak, or several, to one curve.

peak_fit(x, y, shape, fwhm_seed=None)

Fit shape plus a sloping line to one curve.

The model is height * peak(x; center, fwhm) + slope * x + offset, five parameters. The background is always sloping and never optional: on the rocking curves in TiSe2 (32 to 38) it runs 1754 counts down to 476 across scan 32 alone, and fitting it lifts the mean R2 of the whole curve from 0.957 to 0.980 for a Gaussian -- 0.976 for a flat offset, so it is the slope that is bought and not merely a pedestal. (Those three are measured against the raw curve, which is not how fit_r2 below is reckoned; they compare background models, not fits.) height is measured above that line, which is why it comes out below the curve's own maximum (46664 against 51863 on scan 32).

Every value is None when no trustworthy fit came back, which the table shows as a blank row and the export writes as (none). That happens a great deal more often than it might seem it should, and deliberately: see the gate below.

fwhm_seed is curve_stats' crossing width when it found one. It is passed in rather than recomputed because the caller has just worked it out for the same curve.

fit_columns(mixed)

The fit headings, the mixing's among them only where it is fitted.

One function so that the headings and the cells under them cannot be built from two different ideas of the order -- _set_stats_columns and _update_stats_table both come here.

peak_fit_multi(x, y, shape, peaks)

Fit peaks copies of shape over one shared sloping line.

The model is sum(height_k * peak(x; centre_k, fwhm_k)) plus slope * x + offset: three parameters a component and two for the line, which the components share because one background under several peaks is what the data has.

Beside the keys peak_fit fills, this adds fit_peaks -- a list of one row per component, **ordered by centre**. That ordering is what makes a trend across scans mean anything: "peak 1" is then always the leftmost, and its line follows the same physical peak through a set of repeats even where the two swap which is the taller.

The four per-peak values on the returned dict itself stay None, so the curve's own row shows five blank cells and the numbers appear on the peak rows underneath it. fit_slope/fit_offset are kept, because the shared line is what peak_model needs to draw the sum.

Two things this cannot do, both measured and both in the tooltips.

R2 cannot choose peaks for you. The models are nested, so it only ever rises: on scan 32, a single clean rocking curve, one peak scores 0.97832 and two score 0.99761.

And a component too many does not announce itself by failing. Asked for two, a single peak is divided rather than fitted alongside a rejected spare, and the division need not be lopsided: scan 32 comes back as a broad base (FWHM 0.0914) under a sharp core (0.0347) at much the same place, and neither of those is the 0.0447 that one peak reports and that the crossings agree with. The derivative of a knife edge divides too -- scan 101 grows a negative component at -6.4542 and its main width moves 0.02095 to 0.02340, 12%, which is more than the drift the whole trend exists to show. Every component is therefore given a row of its own and the gate below stays geometric: a broad base is as sane a shape as a peak, and no test here can know it was not wanted.

peak_model(x, shape, stats)

Evaluate over x the fit peak_fit returned in stats.

None when there is no fit to draw, so the caller can ask without checking first. Uses .get throughout because a row that was not the one fitted -- the curve while dY/dX is shown, the derivative while it is not -- and any colour map never carry these keys at all.

A peak_fit_multi result is drawn as the one summed model its components make, not as a line apiece: they were fitted together over a single background, and several lines in one colour over one curve would read as several fits rather than one.

peak_parts(x, shape, stats)

The pieces the fit in stats is made of, for "Parts" to draw.

The fitted background first, then one line a component -- each drawn **on** that background rather than from zero, so a component lies where the data it is claiming lies. From zero they would be readable against each other and nowhere near the curve: scan 109's background is about 800 counts, so every component would float a screen below its own peak.

Empty where there is nothing to add to what peak_model already drew. A single-component fit is its background plus its one peak, so the component line would be the summed model over again, in the same colour, and only the background is worth a line of its own.

da6idb.hkl.convert

Turning detector frames into a cloud of points in reciprocal space.

build_converter(geometry)

The xrayutilities area-detector converter for this geometry.

frame_hkl(geometry, hxrd, frame)

(H, K, L) for every pixel of one frame, each shaped like the detector.

classHklCloud

The reduced point cloud handed to the plotter.

.bounds(self)

((h0, h1), (k0, k1), (l0, l1)) of the points kept.

convert(geometry, first=0, last=None, step=1, threshold=0.0, max_points=2000000, progress=None)

Map the selected frames into (H, K, L), keeping the interesting pixels.

Every pixel of every frame is a point, which for a long scan is far more than can be held or drawn -- 601 frames of 516x516 is 160 million. So each frame is reduced as it is converted: pixels at or below threshold are dropped, and if what survives still exceeds max_points the brightest are kept. Reducing per frame keeps the full set from ever being in memory.

da6idb.hkl.detector

The scattering angle a detector pixel sees, from xrayutilities' geometry.

circle_delta(geometry)

What the delta circle read on each frame.

The angle of the centre pixel, which is what the motor is set to -- not the angle of whatever the detector was actually looking at.

detector_basis(hxrd, detector_angles)

Pixel (0, 0) and one step along each pixel axis, in lab coordinates.

getDetectorPos returns the direction to each pixel with the arm rotations applied. That position is affine in the pixel indices, so these three vectors give any pixel, whole or fractional, exactly.

two_theta_maps(geometry, x, y, hxrd=None)

Scattering angles at pixel coordinates x, y, for every frame.

Returns (maps, index): one array shaped like x per distinct detector position, and which of them each frame uses. Frames that leave the detector where it was share a map, so a scan that never moves it evaluates once however many frames it has and however large the region.

two_theta_at(geometry, x, y, hxrd=None)

The scattering angle at pixel (x, y) on each frame, in degrees.

x and y are detector pixels indexed as the frame is stored, and may be fractional -- a centre of mass usually is. Give one position, or one per frame.

The detector basis is rebuilt only when the circles move, so a scan that leaves the detector still costs one call however many frames it has.

read_transmission(image, master, n_frames, frame_epoch=None)

Attenuator transmission for each frame, or None if nothing records it.

The image file stores one reading per frame, which is what a frame was actually taken through; the master samples at scan points and misses the transients the free-running detector catches, so it is only a fallback.

read_geometry(image, master=None, angles_from_master=False, use_setup=True)

The conversion parameters for image, master first where it has them.

The angles are the exception. The image file records a POSITION with each frame, while the master records one per scan point, on a read-back clock that lags the exposure -- matching those onto the frames shifted every frame of a rocking curve by one whole step, which would move the entire map. So the per-frame values win when they exist; angles_from_master forces the resampled ones, and the window exposes the choice.

grid_to_vti(image, master=None, hkl_range=None, output=None, step=None, width=None, oversample=1.0, first=0, last=None, frame_step=1, max_cells=MAX_CELLS, reuse=True, progress=None, geometry=None, normalize_transmission=None)

A cached .vti is reused when the HKL range, the frame range and the geometry it was made for all match what is being asked for now; otherwise the data is gridded and written. Unlike calling grid_to_vti then load_vti, the cached file is read once.

classLoadedGrids

A few gridded maps held in memory, keyed by the file they came from.

Reading a .vti back is about a second for a full-resolution map, and that is the whole cost of flipping between files in a viewer or sweeping planes through one. What is held is keyed by how the file looked when it was read -- path, modification time and size -- so a regrid from somewhere else is noticed rather than served stale.

The budget is in cells, not files: a folder of small maps keeps many of them, a folder of large ones keeps few. The most recently used is never dropped, however large, since it is the one on screen.

What is stored is opaque. A caller that must keep something alongside the grid -- the measured range from make_drawable, which cannot be recomputed once the NaN has gone -- stores the pair.

.stamp(path)

What says a file on disk is still the one that was read.

.get(self, path)

What was held for path, or None if nothing current is.

.put(self, path, value, cells)

Hold value for path, dropping the oldest to stay in budget.

.clear(self)

Let go of everything held, for a caller watching its memory.

make_drawable(grid, fill=0.0)

Replace NaN in a loaded grid so a volume mapper can draw it.

NaN marks cells the scan never reached. Handed to a GPU volume mapper it does not come out transparent -- it lights the whole empty region up -- so it has to become a value at the bottom of the colour scale, where the opacity ramp starts at zero.

Edits the array in place, which on a large grid saves copying a few hundred megabytes, and returns (low, high) of the measured cells read before the NaN goes. The file on disk still holds the NaN.

to_xarray(grid, name='intensity')

A gridded map as an DataArray with real H/K/L coordinates.

The same numbers as the .vti, but labelled: cube.sel(L=7.52, method="nearest").plot() is then a one-liner, and slicing by coordinate beats remembering which index is which. Unmeasured cells stay NaN, which is what xarray and matplotlib both expect of missing data.

xarray is imported here rather than at the top so the rest of the package stays usable without it.

da6idb.hkl.slice

Cutting a plane through a gridded map.

plane_basis(normal)

Two in-plane directions for normal, preferring the HKL axes.

When the normal is along an axis the other two axes span the plane exactly, and they are returned in H, K, L order so an L slice is drawn H across and K up. Otherwise any orthonormal pair will do.

plane_origin(centre, normal, offset=0.0, point=None)

Where the cutting plane sits, from an offset or from a point on it.

An offset is measured from centre along the unit normal, which is fine for sweeping a plane through a map but awkward when you already know the H, K, L you want to cut through: naming the point places the plane directly, and the plane is then the one through it.

Returns (origin, offset) either way -- the second being how far the plane ended up from centre -- so a viewer can always show both descriptions of the same plane, whichever was typed.

slab_offsets(layers)

Offsets, in cells, of a slab of layers planes centred on zero.

sample_volume(grid, points)

Trilinear values of grid at arbitrary HKL points; NaN off the grid.

average_along_normal(grid, points, normal, layers=1, step=None)

Average grid over a slab of layers cells centred on points.

A plane has no thickness, so a slice of one cell is as noisy as the data gets. Averaging a few cells either side along the normal trades a little resolution across the plane for a great deal less noise in it.

line_cut(grid, start, end, samples=None, width=1, normal=None, step=None)

Intensity along the segment from start to end, both in HKL.

The two points name the cut outright, which is what you want when the line runs between features whose H, K, L you know -- dragging one into place by eye cannot be written down or repeated.

width averages that many parallel lines, spaced step apart and centred on the segment, the way a slab average thickens a plane. Their offset direction is perpendicular to the line and, when normal is given, inside that plane, so widening a cut on a slice stays on the slice instead of wandering out of it.

Returns (distance, points, values): distance from start in HKL units, the (n, 3) H, K, L of each sample, and the intensity there, NaN wherever the scan did not reach.

sample_plane(grid, normal, origin, pixels=None, layers=1)

Resample grid on the plane. Returns (image, us, vs, u, v, origin).

image is indexed [row, column], columns running along u and rows along v; us and vs are the sample positions as distances from origin along each. Points off the grid come back NaN. With layers above one the plane is averaged over that many cells along its normal.

read_frame_positions(master, n_frames=None)

Where the motors were for each detector frame, from the master.

A fly scan does not stop at its points, so the detector and the bluesky stream do not agree on how many there are: on the example scan the stream holds 120 points and the detector wrote 170 frames, 121 of which the writer marks in_scan. Matching the one onto the other by time or by position is guesswork, and this is the writer saying outright where each frame was taken.

Every numeric column under FRAME_POSITIONS_PATH comes back under FRAME_PREFIX, booleans as 0 and 1 so they can be plotted and divided by like anything else -- in_scan as a normalisation is how you drop the turnaround frames.

Empty when the master has no such group, which is every scan written before it existed.

read_scan_channels(image, frame_epoch, n_frames=None, master=None)

The master file's primary fields, resampled onto the image's frames.

The detector free-runs, so the two files rarely have the same number of points -- 103 frames against 101 scan points is typical, the camera starting a moment early. Each frame therefore takes the value of the scan point nearest it in time; frames outside the scan hold the first or last point.

When the image file carries no per-frame timestamp at all, there is no clock to match against, so frames are instead spread evenly across the master's scan points by position (see evenly_spaced_index) -- a rougher alignment, but the best available without a time base.

The points of a two-motor scan where they were actually measured.

For a plan whose grid is not a lattice. A fly grid steps one axis and flies the other, so the flown positions differ from row to row -- and so do the row lengths. On the example scans one row holds 11 points and the next 12, the rows alternate direction, and 125 points come back where the plan declared 121; two others declared 441 and recorded 372 and 377. There is no rectangle to reshape into. What there is, is a set of (x, y, value) where the motors were, and that is what this returns -- nothing is snapped to a lattice or resampled, because the irregularity is the measurement rather than noise in it.

clipped_limits(z, percent=COLOR_CLIP_PERCENT)

Colour limits leaving out the most extreme percent at each end.

A handful of wild cells -- a spot where the beam ran off the sample, a detector glitch -- stretch a map's colour scale over a range nothing else occupies, leaving the rest of it one flat shade. Cutting the scale back to where the bulk of the data lives brings that structure back; the cells outside simply saturate.

How much to cut is asked for rather than worked out, which is the point. Rules that decide for themselves what is an outlier (Tukey's fences on the interquartile range, say) read the top of a sharp peak as one, because a peaked map legitimately has a long tail above its upper quartile. On the sample-position rasters this beamline lines up on -- huber_euler_z by huber_euler_y against a detector total, where the peak is the result -- those fences clip most of the peak away: measured over the twelve of them, 3x IQR left scan 468 with 7% of its height, 232 with 14%, 228 with 32%. A number someone typed can be wrong, but it cannot be surprising, and the same number gives the same answer on every map it is applied to.

percent is a percentile, so what it costs depends on how the values are distributed, not on how extreme they are: 0.5 on a 1500-cell raster is the eight cells at each end whatever they hold. Nothing stops it from being large -- a map still washed out after a fraction of a percent may well need several -- but past a few percent it is no longer only spikes going.

Returns the two limits and which ends they actually cut, in the wording colorbar wants for its extend arrows, so a clipped scale says on the bar that it is clipped. (None, None, "neither") means leave the limits alone.

lone_dropouts(monitor)

Mask the points where the beam was gone for that point alone.

A dropout is a point whose monitor has collapsed to less than BEAM_GONE_FRACTION of the column's own median. Every channel goes with it -- on scan 239's row 367 the monitor reads 5372 against a median of 1.85e6, LockDC 5502 against 713000 -- so the point carries no measurement at all, and in the reduction it drags a whole energy bin with it: 239's XMuCD spans 0.511 with that row in and 0.0262 with it out.

Unlike clipped_limits above this needs no amount typed into it, and the difference is worth stating because the two sit side by side. The clip is a judgement -- where a peak stops being signal is not written in the data. This is not: a monitor measures the beam arriving, and a beam that is arriving at 2% of normal has gone. The two populations are forty apart with nothing between them, so the threshold is a reading rather than a choice.

Only isolated dropouts are masked -- both neighbours must be live. A run of them is not a spike but a dump, and the scan should show it: of the 24 scans in these directories carrying a dead point, 17 carry nothing but single ones and 7 were taken while the beam was down, scan 244 for 265 consecutive rows. Deleting that would hide a quarter of the run behind a curve that looked fine. Length, not depth, is what tells the two apart -- scan 89's one dead row is 5% of a 20-point scan and still a spike.

Neighbours are the rows either side in acquisition order, so this wants one file's column at a time: run two files together and the join would stand one scan's last row next to another's first.

What this deliberately will not catch is a spike the monitor knew nothing about. Reduced index 80 of scans 239 and 242 (E = 7.26907) is a 13-sigma one-point excursion with the monitor at 0.91 of median and all four raw rows agreeing to four decimals -- a monochromator glitch, reproduced in every repeat. It is data, and it stays.

scale_extend(z, low, high)

Which ends of z fall outside (low, high), as colour-bar arrows.

In the wording colorbar wants for extend, so a scale that does not reach its own data says so on the bar rather than leaving it to be noticed. None for a limit is matplotlib's "fit the data", which by definition cuts nothing off at that end.

Asked separately from the limits because the limits move after they are first worked out: centered_limits widens a range, and cells that saturated against the clipped one need not saturate against the wider. An arrow left over from the narrower scale points at data that is not out there.

centered_limits(z, low, high)

(low, high) widened until zero sits exactly in the middle.

A diverging colour map is read by where its middle colour falls, and left to the data that lands wherever the range happens to centre. On scan 156's XMCD the clipped range is -0.0009 to +0.0036, which puts white at +0.0013: a map with signal everywhere, drawn so that most of it reads as none. Zero is where white belongs, because zero is what the map is a difference from.

Widening to whichever end reaches further costs the shorter end some of its contrast, and buys a map that can be read without consulting the bar. Either limit may be None, meaning the data's own extreme.

Only ever widens, so no cell that was inside the scale leaves it -- but the arrows still have to be recomputed, which is scale_extend.

Takes either shape of map: a lattice from read_grid, whose z is a rectangle indexed by row and column, or the points as measured from read_scatter, where x, y and z run alongside each other and the position is simply read off at the same index.

trend_choices(peaks=1, mixed=False)

What a trend can follow, as {label: (component, fit key)}.

One peak offers the fit's own numbers and component is None. Two or more offer each component's separately -- "Peak 1 center" and so on -- because that is the question a multi-peak trend is asked: not how the fit as a whole drifted but how each peak did.

The components are numbered as peak_fit_multi returns them, in order of centre, so "Peak 1" is the leftmost in every scan. That is what makes a trend of it mean anything: the line follows one peak through the set rather than jumping whenever two swap which is the taller.
